Unearth the Treasures

Treasures

The Folk Heritage Museum offers a journey back in time, immersing visitors in the medieval era. The centuries-old priceless artifacts within highlight various significant aspects of Bhutanese life in the past.

The Heritage

A Voyage Into Bhutan's Past

Embark on an enchanting journey into Bhutan's past, a voyage akin to turning the pages of a storied history book. Yet here, the narrative unfurls through the visual splendor of antique pots and pans, ancient weapons, venerable weighing scales, and traditional garments.

The air is rich with the aroma of a medieval kitchen, and the sanctity of spaces where ancestors invoked deities enhances the immersive experience. No sojourn through Bhutanese culture is complete without a visit to the Heritage.

Ceremonial Hat of His Majesty Jigme Dorji Wangchuck

Brown brocade, hand-embroidered

Regalia

The ceremonial hat of the Third King of Bhutan, His Majesty Jigme Dorji Wangchuck, made of brown brocade and featuring a hand-embroidered double-crossed Thunderbolt (Dorji Jadram) motif at the front. Round in form, with upward-facing lappets shaped as lotus petals, long blue brocade lappets extend from both sides of the central motif, each adorned with four hanging fabric strips, and the top is finished with a silver metal ornament. His Majesty wore it during ceremonial occasions.

Six Symbols of Longevity (Tshering Drukor)

Traditional composition, painted

Ritual Object

The Six Symbols of Longevity represent six elements associated with long life and harmony in nature: humans (Mi-Tshering), rocks (Dra-Tshering), water (Chu-Tshering), trees (Shing-Tshering), deer (Shou-Tshering), and birds (Ja-Tshering). Tradition associates the symbols with a great master who meditated in a rocky cave, sustained by the shade of a tree and water from a nearby waterfall, living in harmony with a deer and a bird — a harmonious relationship believed to have supported his spiritual practice and attainment of enlightenment.

Traditional Weighing Scales

Sang, Goe & Tiru Sangchung

Measurement

Three standard weighing scales once used across Bhutanese households. The Sang measured smaller quantities of meat, butter, cheese, vegetables and seeds — equivalent to one phita of grain, with three sang to a kilogram, so one sang weighs roughly 333.32 grams. The Goe, used for larger quantities of meat and grain, is equivalent to six sang. The Tiru Sangchung, used by silversmiths to measure gold, silver, brass, copper and mercury, is calibrated against the double-edged Victorian silver coin from British India (locally, Ngitsem) — each mark on the beam representing one coin's weight, roughly 11.66 grams, a unit known locally as one tiru or tola.

Palang / Jandom / Ara-rou

Wood, bamboo or horn

Household Object

The Palang is a traditional Bhutanese wine container, commonly made from wood, bamboo, or animal horns, used to store and serve Ara — a locally brewed alcoholic beverage that holds an important place in Bhutanese social, cultural and ceremonial practices. Traditionally, Ara is offered to guests as a gesture of hospitality and used as an offering to local deities during religious and cultural ceremonies.

Baking Pan of Pemalingpa

Cast metal, 15th–16th century

Historical Artifact

This pan holds significant historical and cultural importance, traditionally attributed to the revered Bhutanese treasure revealer (terton) Pema Lingpa (1450–1521). Raised by his blacksmith grandfather, Pema Lingpa learned various traditional crafts and became skilled in both arts and religious practice. Pans of this type were used to prepare buckwheat pancakes, a specialty and staple food of Bumthang — this particular pan is considered auspicious due to the spiritual blessing traditionally believed to have been imparted by Pema Lingpa.

Carved Rabsel Window

Cypress, hand-carved

Woodwork

A traditional rabsel bay window, its lattice carved from a single cypress panel and left unpainted to show the tool marks of the carpenter. Windows of this type project from the upper storey of a house and were reserved for the family shrine room or the head of household's quarters.

Hilltop Chorten Model

Lime render over stone, votive scale

Ritual Object

A votive chorten built at reduced scale, its whitewashed lime render and gilded finial modelled on the reliquary stupas that mark ridgelines across the valley. Small chortens like this are commissioned to accumulate merit on behalf of a household, and are sited deliberately — this one facing the same ridge as the temple it was modelled on.

Monastic Robe

Wool, natural dye

Ritual Object

A maroon monastic robe, dyed in small batches using madder root and walnut husk. The deep, slightly uneven colour is characteristic of natural dye lots — no two robes from the same batch are ever identical, which is part of why they are valued over synthetic alternatives.

Guardian Mask

Carved wood, gesso & pigment

Ritual Object

A carved wooden guardian mask, coated in gesso and painted in mineral pigment, worn during the black-hat cham to represent protective deities. Masks of this scale are carved from a single block of willow or cypress, hollowed from the reverse to reduce weight for the dancer.

Ceremonial Kabney

Raw silk, handwoven

Textile

A handwoven silk scarf, worn draped over the shoulder as a mark of rank and occasion. The particular weave and undyed cream tone shown here identifies the wearer's status within a formal gathering — a system of colour and cloth that predates any written record of it.
